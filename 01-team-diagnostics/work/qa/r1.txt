and the method notes.
What I took away from this part, in one paragraph.
Scoring rose by 0.58 runs an over between the seasons, and the rise was
not even: the powerplay and the death overs went up a lot, the middle
overs much less. The middle overs are where spin does its work, and spin
is the cheaper option there in both seasons. When I lined up every
team-season against its win percentage, the single habit most closely
tied to winning was taking wickets in the middle overs
(r = 0.69). Grounds differ by almost two runs an over, so any team
comparison has to hold the venue fixed. And at the auction, every squad
costs roughly the same; what differs is how much of the money is new.
Those five observations shape everything in Part 2.

1.3 How complete the
tracking fields are
[IMG]
Share of deliveries with each tracking field filled in, by season.
Line, length and shot are filled on more than 99% of balls; speed and
release point on about 93%. Just as important, the coverage barely moves
between seasons (the largest change is 0.7 points). If a field had been
recorded much more often in one year, a “change” in that field would
partly be a change in recording. Here that worry can be set aside. One
field cannot: bowl_type changes meaning between seasons, so
bowler type is taken from the bowling-style column instead.
2 How a T20 innings is
shaped
2.1 Over by over
[IMG]
League runs per over (top) and wickets per 100 legal balls (bottom), by
over, innings and season.
The innings has the shape every follower of the game knows: a fast
start once the field opens up, a dip in overs 7 to 10 when the fielders
go back and spin comes on, then a steady climb to the death. Wickets
follow a similar curve, rising sharply in the last three overs, when
batters swing at everything.
What changed in 2024 is the level, not the shape. The league scored
8.94 an over in 2023 and 9.52 in 2024. The biggest jump is in the chase
powerplay, where teams were clearly more willing to go hard from ball
one. 2024 was the first full season with the impact-player rule, which
gives every side an extra batter, and the numbers look exactly like
that: more risk at the top, because there is more batting behind.
What this means for the analysis. Any model that
pools the two seasons without a season term will rate every 2024 batter
above average and every 2024 bowler below, which is a statement about
the rules rather than the players. Every comparison in Part 2 is made
within season.
2.2 By phase
[IMG]
League profile by phase: runs per over, wickets per over, boundary
percentage and dot-ball percentage.
The three phases behave like three different games. The powerplay has
the most dot balls (two fielders out, so bowlers attack the stumps) but
also a high boundary rate. The middle overs have the lowest boundary
rate and the most “quiet” cricket. The death overs have the highest run
rate and twice the wicket rate of the middle overs.
Between the seasons the powerplay rose by 0.75 an over and the death
by 0.74, but the middle overs by only 0.41. The middle overs held their
value even as scoring inflated elsewhere, which already hints that a
team which controls them has something scarce.
2.3 Totals
[IMG]
Distribution of innings totals by innings and season. Dashed lines are
medians; the dotted line marks 200.
The average first-innings total went from 184 to 193, and the number
of 200-plus totals from 24 to 26. The shift is not just a move of the
centre; the right tail got much longer, with totals in the 250s and 260s
that would have been freakish a few seasons ago. Chasing sides won 45%
of games in 2023 and 51% in 2024.
3 Context: toss, grounds
and conditions
3.1 The toss
[IMG]
Share of matches won by the chasing side, split by what the toss winner
chose.
Captains chose to field in most matches, the standard T20 instinct
(you know the target, and dew helps the batting side at night). It did
not buy much. The side that chased won roughly half its games in both
seasons, and the toss winner did no better than the toss loser. The toss
is a weak lever, and I leave it out of the team analysis.
3.2 Grounds
[IMG]
Pace economy against spin economy at each ground with four or more
matches. The dotted line is equality.
Grounds matter a lot. Eden Gardens ran at 9.91 an over and Ekana at
8.14, a gap of 1.8 runs an over, or about 35 runs across an innings.
Chinnaswamy in Bengaluru, a small ground at altitude, is among the
highest-scoring venues; Chepauk and Ekana, with slower pitches, are the
lowest. Every point on the chart sits above the line: spin was
cheaper than pace at every ground, even the batting
paradises.
What this means for the analysis. A team that plays
seven games a season at a high-scoring home ground will look worse with
the ball and better with the bat on raw numbers. Any team comparison has
to hold the ground fixed. Part 2 does this twice: inside the
expected-runs model and again with ground fixed effects in a
regression.
4 Bowling
4.1 Pace and spin by
phase
[IMG]
Left: spin’s share of balls bowled by phase. Right: economy of pace and
spin by phase and season.
Spin is a middle-overs weapon: it bowled 62% of middle-overs balls in
2023. In 2024 teams bowled less spin everywhere (54% in the middle
overs), probably because the extra batter from the impact-player rule
made spinners easier to target. But in the middle overs spin was still
the cheaper option: 8.22 an over against 9.29 for pace. At the death in
2024 spin was taken apart (10.98 an over), so the spinners are best used
in overs 7 to 15.
4.2 Where to land the
ball
[IMG]
Runs per over (top number) and wickets per 100 balls (bottom number) for
every line and length combination with 30 or more balls, pace and spin.
The heatmap is a reminder of the basics. For pace, the yorker on the
stumps goes for 4.9 an over and takes a wicket every ten balls; full
tosses and short balls outside the off stump are the most expensive
deliveries in the game. For spin, a good length on the stumps or just
outside off is the stock ball, and it is cheap. Bowling short or too
full as a spinner is punished hard.
4.3 How bowling
changed
[IMG]
Share of balls by length for pace and spin, each season.
In 2024 the IPL allowed two bouncers an over, and the data show it:
pace bowlers bowled noticeably more short balls and fewer
back-of-a-length balls. Spinners pushed a little fuller. These are the
kind of league-wide shifts that make within-season comparison
necessary.
4.4 How wickets fall
[IMG]
How wickets fell in each phase.
About seven wickets in ten are catches in every phase. Stumpings only
appear when spinners bowl, and run-outs roughly triple at the death as
batters scramble for twos.
5 Batting
5.1 Shots: risk and
reward
[IMG]
Strike rate against the share of balls played in control, for each shot.
Colour is the dismissal rate.
The shots line up along a clear trade-off. The slog sweep has the
highest strike rate of any shot (278) but gets the batter out 16% of the
time; the slog itself is similar. The straight drive is the outlier: a
strike rate of 276 with 88% control, because it is only played to the
right ball. The defensive shots sit at the bottom, safe and almost
scoreless.
5.2 Where the runs
go
[IMG]
Share of runs off the bat by scoring zone, from the batter’s point of
view (left-handers mirrored). Bowler at the top.
Midwicket is the busiest zone in the league for both hands. Against
spin, it takes 27% of all runs, and midwicket plus mid-on together take
nearly half: batters hit with the turn or slog across the line. Against
pace, runs are spread more evenly, with far more coming behind square on
both sides (fine leg, third man) from pace on the ball.
[IMG]
Direction of every four and six, batter-relative, in 15-degree bins.
The boundary rose makes the same point sharper. Sixes off spin go
almost entirely to the leg side and straight. Sixes off pace go leg side
too, but more behind square, from pulls and hooks. Fours are spread
round the wicket. A captain setting a field for a spinner in the middle
overs should expect most of the damage between mid-on and deep square
leg.
5.3 Matchups
[IMG]
Strike rate and balls per dismissal for each batter hand against each
bowling style (300+ balls).
Matchups are real, and they point the way the coaching manuals say.
The hardest balls to score off are the ones turning away from
the bat: off-spin to left-handers (strike rate 123, a wicket every 31
balls) and left-arm orthodox to right-handers (127). Medium pace is the
easiest to score off for everyone. This is why a balanced spin attack,
with balls turning both ways, matters in the middle overs.
5.4 Getting in
[IMG]
Strike rate by balls already faced in the innings, each season.
A batter’s first five balls go at a strike rate around 112; by ten
balls in he is scoring at 150 and up. Kartikeya Date’s point, that a
batter on 0 off 0 and the same batter on 30 off 20 are different
players, is visible in the raw data, and it is why “balls already faced”
goes into the expected-runs model in Part 2. In 2024 batters got going
faster at every stage.
6 Teams
6.1 Raw team
profiles
[IMG]
Runs per over by phase for every team-season, batting (top) and conceded
(bottom). Green is better than that season’s league average.
No team is good everywhere, and the weak spots move from season to
season. This is a raw view (no adjustment for ground or match
situation), so it is a starting point, not a verdict. A few things
already stand out. Kolkata’s 2024 batting is green in every phase, which
matches their title. Delhi’s 2023 batting is red everywhere. Bengaluru’s
middle-overs bowling is among the most expensive in both seasons, while
their powerplay and death bowling are not. Part 2 will check whether
these survive the adjustments.
6.2 What goes with
winning
[IMG]
Correlation of each team-level rate with win percentage across the 20
team-seasons. Each rate is centred within its season first.
I lined up fourteen team-level rates against win percentage across
the twenty team-seasons (centring each rate within its season first, so
that 2024’s higher scoring doesn’t count as “better”). The strongest
relationship is bowling wickets per over in the middle
overs (r = 0.69), ahead of middle-overs batting run rate (0.50)
and not losing early wickets with the bat (-0.50).
This is twenty points, so I would not read much into the exact order,
and correlation is not cause. But it fits the cricket. The middle overs
are the longest phase (nine overs), and a wicket there both stops the
run rate and exposes the lower order before the death. A team that
cannot take middle-overs wickets lets the opposition reach over 15 with
wickets in hand, and that is exactly when the 2024 batting became most
destructive.
7 Players
[IMG]
Batters with 100+ balls in a season: average against strike rate. The
eight busiest batters each season are labelled.
[IMG]
Bowlers with 100+ balls in a season: economy (reversed) against wickets
per 100 balls.
The individual landscape is wide, and two seasons are a small sample
for any one player. A bowler with 150 balls in a season has bowled 25
overs; one bad night moves his economy by half a run. This is why Part 2
never ranks players on raw rates. It compares each ball with what an
average player would have done in the same situation, and then shrinks
each player’s record towards the average for his role, by an amount the
data choose (Part 3 explains how).
8 The auction market
8.1 What squads cost
[IMG]
Squad cost by team and season, split into money for retained (or traded)
players and money spent at the auction.
Squads cost roughly the same, because the purse cap binds. What
differs is how much of the money is new. Before 2023, RCB spent
only ₹7.0 crore at the auction, the least in the league, and kept almost
everyone. Before 2024 they spent ₹39.7 crore, the most. That swing is a
decision, and Part 2 audits it.
8.2 What the market pays
for
[IMG]
Contract value by role and origin (log scale).
Overseas players cost more at the median in most roles, which
reflects the four-overseas limit on any XI. But inside each role the
spread is enormous, from base price to the top contract. Price is a weak
guide to role, and (as Part 2 shows) only a moderate guide to value.
8.3 How much squads
changed
[IMG]
Players kept, released and newly arrived between the 2023 and 2024
auction sheets.
Most teams kept 13 to 18 players. Sunrisers changed the most (12
arrivals). Bengaluru released nine and brought in nine.

The exploration doesn’t pick a team, and I didn’t want it to. What it
does is set the rules for picking one fairly: compare within season,
hold the ground fixed, split by phase and by bowler type, demand that a
weakness shows up in both seasons, and look at what the money did. Part 2 turns those rules into a
screen.
