Abstract
Data Premier League 2026, preliminary round, Task 1. The task has two
problems inside it. Problem A: which franchise should a scouting report
be about? Problem B: for that franchise, what is wrong, what did it do
about it, and what should it do next? I answer A with a screen every
team goes through, using three gates fixed in advance; only one team
passes all three. I answer B with a separate set of hypotheses and a
decision audit that follows the franchise season by season, from its
2023 data to the December 2023 auction to its 2024 results, and puts a
number in runs, wins and crore on each step. Everything is in R and
every number in the text is computed.

The short version.
Which team. I did not pick a franchise and then look for its
problems. Every team went through the same three gates: did it win fewer
games than its runs deserved in both seasons; does it
have a weakness on the field that survives a multiple-testing
correction, a ground adjustment and a second season; and did it pay more
for its squad than its wins were worth. Only Royal Challengers
Bengaluru passes all three.
What is wrong. Most of their reputation for bad bowling is
their home ground. What is left, and what repeats in both seasons, is
spin in the middle overs: -27.9 runs per 100 balls
against the rest of the league, the worst cell of any team. It cost
about 105 runs, or 1.2 wins, in 2023 and the same again in 2024.
What they did. After 2023 the data pointed at spin. At the
December 2023 auction RCB released both of their main spinners, spent
₹36.2 crore of new money on pace and ₹2.0 crore on spin, and the
middle-overs spin cell was no better in 2024 (-26.8 and then -29.0).
Their 2024 turnaround, from 1 win in 7 to 6 in 7, came from the
powerplay, not from the weak cell.
What next. Buy one Indian middle-overs spinner whose rate is
in the league’s top quarter, with a bid ceiling set from projected wins,
and move middle overs to the bowlers who are already good at them. Don’t
spend the big money on more pace or on “clutch”.
1 The task, and how I
approached it
The brief asks for a scouting report on one franchise across two
seasons: diagnose the problem and suggest strategies. Read closely, that
is two problems, and they need different methods.
Problem A: which team? A choice among ten. It needs
a fair screen, so that the team is the output of an analysis and not an
input to it.
Problem B: that team’s problem. A diagnosis and a
set of decisions. It needs its own hypotheses, its own tests and a way
to put a value on each decision.
I worked through both using the same six steps: describe the
situation, search what others have
already learned, name the obvious gap, choose a
representation that ties the pieces together, write
down hypotheses before testing them, and arrive at the
key question. I borrowed this sequence from a
problem-solving course, because it forces the order that analysis
usually skips: you are not allowed to pick the answer first.
[IMG]
The six steps, as applied to this task.
1.1 Situation
A franchise cannot change the last two seasons. It can change three
things: who bowls and bats when (deployment),
who is in the squad (retention, trades and the auction)
and what it pays (price discipline). An analysis that
does not end in one of those three is description, not decision support.
Amol Desai put it more simply in the mentor session: start at the
decision, name the kind of question you are asking, and only then pick a
model.

1.3 The obvious gap
Part 1 showed that grounds, seasons and phases all move raw numbers a
lot, and that most team weak spots move between seasons. The obvious gap
is therefore not “which team was worst” (the table already says that)
but which team under-achieved relative to its own cricket, in a
way that repeats and that a franchise could act on. That is a
narrower and more useful question, and it is the one Problem A
answers.
1.4 Representation: the
problem map
[IMG]
The problem map for Task 1. Every analysis in this report measures one
of these links.
The map is how I hold the whole task in my head. Money (purse and
market) buys a squad. The squad is deployed into three phases, under
conditions set by the ground. Phases produce runs for and against. Runs
turn into wins, through how well a team converts close games. And wins,
set against what the squad cost, give value per crore, which feeds back
into the next auction. A franchise can only pull the levers on the left
(purse, market, squad, deployment). Everything on the right is an
outcome. So a useful diagnosis has to trace an outcome on the right back
to a lever on the left.
Each link has a measurement attached: the hedonic price model for the
market, runs above expected for the phases, the Pythagorean link from
runs to wins, win probability for conversion, and WAR for value. Section
3 builds those tools, one after another, each one feeding the next.

1.6 The key
questions
Problem A. Which franchise lost more games than its
cricket deserved, in both seasons, with a cause on the field that
survives scrutiny and a money problem that reaches the front office?
Problem B. For that franchise: which part of its
cricket was persistently below what an average side would have produced
in the same situations; did its auction decisions address that; and what
is the cheapest change that would recover it now?
2 Problem A: choosing the
team
2.1 Why one dimension is
not enough
There are two easy ways to choose a team, and I tried both in earlier
versions of this project.
The first is a single composite score: standardise a few measures and
add them up. It works, but it hides why a team came top, and
the weights are a matter of taste. My first version averaged a results
residual with a money residual and put Punjab top; the money link turned
out to have almost no fit, so I was averaging a real signal with
noise.
The second is to scan one dimension in depth. The all-teams engine I
built earlier (in Ext/Task 1) scanned every phase ×
discipline × pace/spin cell for every team and put each team’s worst
cell through three hurdles. It was rigorous and it said “no single
dominant defect” for half the league, which is what a method that is not
manufacturing stories should say. But it found two teams with a
“structural” defect and had nothing to say about results or money.
So this screen keeps the depth of the cell scan and adds the two
things it lacked. Every team is described on every
dimension, and the choice runs through three gates,
each of which has to be passed on its own. Nothing is added up.
2.2 Gate 1: results
against what the runs deserved
Given the runs a team scored and conceded, how many games should it
have won? The Pythagorean model answers that: expected win share is
\(R^k / (R^k + A^k)\), with the
exponent fitted on all twenty team-seasons rather than borrowed from
baseball.
[IMG]
Actual win percentage against Pythagorean expectation for every
team-season.
The fitted exponent is k = 7.92, and expectation explains 54% of the
variation in win percentage (mean absolute error 7.4 points). That is
enough fit for the gap between actual and expected to mean
something.
2 teams were below expectation in both seasons: RCB
and PBKS. RCB won -5.4 points fewer than expected in 2023 and -10.9 in
2024, about 2.4 wins short over the two seasons. They outscored their
opponents overall and still won fewer than half their games.
[IMG]
How often each team stays below expectation in both seasons when its
matches are resampled.
I want to be straight about how firm this is. Resampling each team’s
matches 1,000 times, RCB stay below expectation in both seasons only 58%
of the time. Part of any team’s conversion gap is luck in close games
(Section 4 measures how much of RCB’s is). So Gate 1 identifies teams
worth a closer look. It does not, by itself, prove a skill problem. That
is what Gate 2 is for.
2.3 Gate 2: a cause on
the field that survives scrutiny
For each team, the screen splits the game into 12 cells (batting and
bowling × powerplay, middle, death × pace and spin) and measures each
one in runs above average per 100 balls, from the
team’s point of view, against the other nine teams. That measure comes
from the expected-runs model in Section 3: every ball is compared with
what an average player would have produced at that ground, in that over,
with those wickets down, against that type of bowling. So a cell’s
number already has the ground and the match situation taken out.
A team’s worst cell then has to clear three hurdles, each guarding
against a different way of fooling yourself:
[TABLE]
[IMG]
Every team, every cell. Numbers are runs above the rest of the league
per 100 balls; stars count hurdles cleared.
[IMG]
Each team’s most defensible weakness and how many hurdles it clears.
Out of 83 team-cells with enough balls to judge, one
clears all three hurdles: RCB’s middle-overs spin bowling, at -27.9 runs
per 100 balls (95% interval -48.5 to -5.7). It has a Benjamini-Hochberg
q of 0.061, keeps -23.0 per 100 balls with ground fixed effects (p =
0.001), and is -26.8 in 2023 and -29.0 in 2024. 4 teams have a cell that
clears two hurdles, and 5 have nothing that clears more than one.
This agrees with the old all-teams engine, which used raw economy
rather than runs above average and also put RCB’s middle-overs spin at
the top of its list. Two independently built methods landing on the same
cell is reassuring.
One sensitivity check, because thresholds are choices. RCB’s q is
0.061. At a stricter 5% false-discovery rate no team would clear Gate 2,
and the screen would fall back to the team passing the most gates with
the strongest cell, which is still RCB. The choice is not sitting on a
knife edge.
2.4 Gate 3: money
From the value model in Section 3, every player-season has a WAR
(wins above replacement), and the league has a going rate for a win:
what all franchises together paid above the minimum, divided by the wins
they got for it. A team’s surplus is the market value
of its wins minus what it paid.
[IMG]
Squad spend on players who played against squad WAR, with the league’s
crore-per-WAR rate drawn as lines.
5 teams had a negative surplus over the two seasons. RCB’s was −₹11.7
crore, the most negative in the league, driven by 2023, when they paid
₹8.81 crore per win against a league rate of ₹6.15.
[IMG]
Squad spend against net run rate, 20 team-seasons.
Hypothesis S4 holds: at team level, what a squad cost explains little
about how it played (r = 0.36, p = 0.12). Squad WAR, built ball by ball
without ever looking at a result, tracks win percentage far better (r =
0.70). If money buys anything, it does not show up in team totals, so
the money question has to be asked at player level, which is what the
decision audit in Section 5 does.

[IMG]
The three gates for every team.
[TABLE]
Royal Challengers Bengaluru is the only team that passes all
three gates, so it is the subject of the rest of this report.
It is worth saying why the obvious alternatives are not:
Punjab passes Gates 1 and 3: they also
under-converted in both seasons and also overpaid. But no cell of theirs
clears more than one hurdle. Their problems were spread out, which makes
them harder to diagnose and harder to fix with one decision.
Mumbai had the worst single season against
expectation (2024), and their middle-overs spin also clears two hurdles.
But they over-achieved in 2023, so the results gap does not
repeat, and their surplus is positive.
Delhi, Gujarat and Lucknow have two-hurdle cells
but won as many or more games than their runs deserved.
Verdicts on the selection hypotheses. S1 supported
(two teams below expectation in both seasons, though neither firmly
under resampling). S2 supported for RCB only. S3 supported for RCB (and
for four others). S4 supported: spend does not explain results at team
level.
3 The measuring
tools
Testing the team’s problem properly needs three things the raw data
do not give directly: an expectation for every ball, so
a ball at Chinnaswamy in the 18th over is compared with balls like it; a
win probability for every ball, so that “pressure” can
be defined from the match state; and a value scale that
puts batting, bowling and price on one axis. Each gets a ladder of
models, from the simplest thing that could work to the most flexible
thing I could defend, trained on 2023 and scored on 2024. A random split
would let a model see half of a match whose other half is in the test
set; worse, it answers the wrong question. Not “can this interpolate?”
but “would it have been any use last April?”
Each tool feeds the next, along the problem map: expected runs give
runs above expected; win probability turns runs and wickets into one
currency; that gives runs per win; runs per win gives WAR; WAR and
prices give value per crore. The detail is in Part 3; here is what each
step found.
3.1 Ladder 1: expected
runs per ball
[TABLE]
[IMG]
Expected-runs ladder, scored on 2024 deliveries.
Boosting is the most accurate (2.8% better than the null on Poisson
deviance); M2 is 2.1% better and is the only model whose calibration
slope is essentially one (1.004). Nobody explains much of a single ball;
cricket deliveries are mostly noise. For a measurement model, where the
residual is the product, calibration matters more than the last
half-percent of accuracy, so M2 is used everywhere
downstream. Its venue factors also give a clean read on the
grounds:
[IMG]
Venue run multipliers from M2, shrunk towards 1.
3.2 Ladder 2: win
probability, WPA and leverage
[IMG]
Out-of-sample log-loss for the win-probability ladder (lower is better).
The best model is W1, the one with the fewest fitted
numbers: a three-parameter resource curve for expected
remaining runs, fed through a Pythagorean link with the exponent fixed
at 6, as in Himanish Ganjoo’s T20 Basics. The logistic and GAM
models with many more parameters were worse on 2024. A parameter pinned
by prior knowledge travelled better than the same parameter learned from
one season. That is Amol Desai’s point about complexity being a cost,
seen in the numbers.
W1 gives win probability added (WPA) for every ball and
leverage, Ganjoo’s p(six) − p(wicket): how much the
next ball could swing the match, known before it is bowled. Leverage is
what defines “pressure” in Section 4, without looking at outcomes.
3.3 Ladder 3: runs to
wins to crore
Runs above average per ball (runs above expected plus a wicket term,
centred within season) roll up into WAR through a
replacement level and a runs-per-win rate, following Rafique’s cricWAR.
Runs per win comes from differentiating the Pythagorean model at parity:
4 × runs per match / k = 90.3 (cricWAR’s published IPL
figure is 84.5). Squad WAR, built without looking at a result,
correlates with wins at r = 0.75.
[IMG]
Squad WAR against wins, 20 team-seasons.
The league paid ₹6.15 crore per WAR in 2023 and ₹5.93 in 2024. By
role and origin, Indian bowlers were the cheapest source of wins and
overseas players the dearest:
[IMG]
Crore paid per win above replacement, by role and origin.
3.4 Ladder 4: what the
auction pays for
A hedonic price model in the style of Malhotra (2022) says overseas
players cost about 170% more than Indian players in the same role and
bowlers about 42% less than batters. Once last season’s WAR is in the
model the market does pay for performance, but price explains only part
of value. Combined with the chart above, the cheapest wins in this
market are Indian bowlers.
[IMG]
Hedonic price models: coefficients on the log-price scale.
3.5 The pressure
layer
Sahen Gupta’s PACE framework (pressure context, arousal signature,
consistency, event sequence) shapes this part, along with his warning to
compare a player with himself. The headline result is short:
clutch is not repeatable in this data. A batter’s
pressure differential in 2023 correlates with his 2024 figure at r =
-0.28 (n = 18). The full layer is in Part 3.
4 Problem B, part 1: what
is wrong with RCB?
4.1 A separate set of
hypotheses
Problem A asked “which team?”. Problem B asks a different question,
so it gets its own register, again written before the tests:
[TABLE]
H1 to H6 are about the cricket. H7 and H8 are about the franchise’s
decisions, and Section 5 tests them.
4.2 H4: is it the
ground?
On raw numbers RCB bowled badly: 9.57 an over conceded against 9.19
for everyone else. That is the familiar story. But Chinnaswamy is small,
high and fast-scoring, and they bowl half their overs there.
[IMG]
Bowling gap to the league, raw and venue-adjusted, at home and away.
In a regression with match-clustered errors, the team’s overall
bowling effect goes from +5.3 to -0.6 runs per 100 balls once ground
fixed effects are added.
H4: supported for bowling
overall. The general “can’t bowl” story is mostly the
ground. That doesn’t make the problem go away; it relocates it. Whatever
is left with the ground held fixed is the real target.
4.3 H1: is the shortfall
concentrated?
[IMG]
Runs above expected per 100 balls by phase and discipline, each season,
with 95% match-bootstrap intervals.
[IMG]
Middle-overs bowling by team and bowler type, both seasons.
With ground and situation held fixed, RCB’s batting is fine
everywhere and their powerplay and death bowling rank 1 and 2 in the
league. One phase stands out: middle-overs bowling, 10th of 10. Split by
bowler type, it is almost entirely spin. RCB’s spinners in overs 7 to 15
were -24.1 runs per 100 balls against expectation, while every other
team’s spinners averaged +3.8; the next-worst team was at -8.2. Their
middle-overs pace, by contrast, went from -15.2 in 2023 to +5.0 in 2024,
which is what a cell without a persistent problem looks like.
The spin split was chosen after seeing that the middle overs were the
worst phase, which is how people find patterns in noise. The screen in
Section 2 deals with that properly: middle-overs spin was one of 12
pre-defined cells for every team, the correction for multiplicity was
applied across all 12, and it replicates out of sample.
Treat 2023 as discovery (-22.9 per 100) and 2024 as the test (-25.3, p =
0.004): the squad changed and the number did not.
H1: supported. The shortfall sits
in middle-overs spin, it is the worst cell in the league, it repeats in
both seasons and it survives the ground adjustment. It cost about 105
runs in 2023 and 103 in 2024 against the rest of the league’s spinners:
roughly 1.2 wins a season at 90 runs per win, or ₹7.0 crore at the
league’s going rate.
4.4 H2 and H3: deployment
or squad?
[IMG]
Every bowler with 24+ middle-overs balls for the team: share of the work
against how good they are there (shrunk towards the average).
The most-used middle-overs bowler was Karn Sharma (17% of the balls),
whose shrunk rate sits in the 2nd percentile of the league. The bowlers
with a positive record there, Cameron Green, Mohammed Siraj and Glenn
Maxwell, got 23% of the work. So there is a deployment problem. But none
of them is a frontline spinner. Of the frontline spinners with a real
share (Karn Sharma, Wanindu Hasaranga and Mayank Dagar), none is above
the league’s median regular middle-overs spinner (shrunk +2.3 per 100).
The best spin option was Glenn Maxwell, a part-timer whose main job is
batting.
H2: partly
supported. Better middle-overs options were under-used,
but they are pace bowlers and part-timers and cannot carry the
phase.
H3: supported. The squad had no
spinner of even median quality for the middle overs. This is a missing
archetype, not a good player having a bad year.
4.5 H5: was the money
where it mattered?
[IMG]
Realised surplus by player and season. Market value is WAR at the
league’s going rate.
In 2023 RCB paid ₹8.81 crore per win above replacement against a
league rate of ₹6.15, with a realised surplus of −₹19.7 crore. In 2024
they improved to ₹5.77 per win and a surplus of ₹8.1 crore, carried by
cheap Indian players who over-delivered. The money lost sits mostly with
expensive overseas players who either did not play much or did not
perform.
H5: supported for 2023,
weaker for 2024. Money sat on the roles with the
market’s highest cost per win, while the archetype the squad lacked is
in the cheapest part of the market.
4.6 H6: is it pressure,
or is it luck?
[IMG]
Win rate in close games against all other games, both seasons.
RCB played 3 close games over the two seasons (a chase still between
25% and 75% at the start of the 18th over) and won 0. In every other
game they won 14 of 26. They lost only 2 of the 16 games in which they
were at least 75% to win, about the league norm. So the results gap is
not a habit of throwing away won games. A good part of it is a handful
of coin-flips that landed the wrong way, plus a bowling cell that kept
games closer than they needed to be. And clutch performance does not
repeat from one season to the next (r = -0.28 for batters).
H6: rejected.
No evidence that RCB’s players come apart under pressure in a way that
would repeat. Paying for “clutch” would be paying for last year’s
luck.
5 Problem B, part 2: what
did the franchise do about it?
The diagnosis says what was wrong across two seasons. A franchise,
though, did not get two seasons at once. It got 2023, then had to
decide, then got 2024. So this section follows the decisions in the
order they were made, and asks of each: what did the data say at the
time, what did the franchise do, how did it turn out, and what would
have been better.
Two rules. Anything labelled foresight uses only
what was available at the time. Anything labelled
hindsight uses later results and is there to grade a
decision, not to pretend the franchise could have known. And nothing
here is causal: a released player doing well elsewhere does not prove he
would have done well at home.
[IMG]
The two seasons as a sequence of decisions.
5.1 Step 1: what 2023
said (foresight)
[IMG]

An analyst looking only at 2023 would have seen two clearly bad
cells: middle-overs spin bowling (-26.8 per 100 balls)
and batting against spin in the powerplay (-19.5, a
small cell of 330 balls over two seasons). The bowling cell was the
bigger and more expensive problem.
[IMG]
What the middle-overs spin gap cost, each season.
In 2023 the gap cost about 105 runs against an average spin attack,
or 1.2 wins, worth about ₹7.2 crore at the going rate. RCB finished one
win short of the play-offs that season.

5.2 Step 2: what the
franchise did at the December 2023 auction
[IMG]
Contract value of players released, kept and brought in for 2024,
grouped by what they bowl.
[TABLE]
RCB released both of their main spinners from 2023 (Shahbaz Ahmed and
Wanindu Hasaranga, ₹13.2 crore of contracts). Releasing Wanindu
Hasaranga was defensible on the numbers: he was the most expensive and
the worst-performing spinner in the cell. But the replacements were
Mayank Dagar and Swapnil Singh, for ₹2.0 crore between them, while ₹36.2
crore of new money went on pace: Alzarri Joseph, Cameron Green, Lockie
Ferguson, Vijaykumar Vyshak, Yash Dayal. The largest single move, the
trade for Cameron Green, was a pace-bowling allrounder.
On the record of 2023 alone, the auction went the other way
from the diagnosis. The weakness was spin in the middle overs;
95% of the new bowling money went on pace.

In 2024 Karn Sharma bowled 39% of the middle-overs spin, at 10.00 an

bowled a minority of the overs, and only Glenn Maxwell was clearly above

5.4 Step 4: what the
market offered (foresight and hindsight)
Was there a spinner to buy? The December 2023 auction pool,
restricted to players who had bowled middle-overs spin in 2023:
[TABLE]
The pool was thin. Only 6 spinners in it had bowled any middle overs
in 2023, and none of them had a long record of doing it well. So “buy a

6 Problem B, part 3: what
next
6.1 The options
The diagnosis points at one archetype, a middle-overs spinner who
controls runs, and one deployment habit. The options follow the order in
which a franchise can act: what is free, what costs money, and what to
stop doing.
Two ways to count the prize. The measured cost of
the problem is about 1.2 wins a season. That describes the past. What a
franchise can expect to recover is smaller, because the players
responsible would be expected to drift back towards average anyway.
Projected on shrunk estimates, the current middle-overs spinners would
run at about -2.6 per 100 balls next season. The decision is made on the
smaller number.

On shrunk projections a top-quartile Indian spinner is worth 0.24
wins a season with a 85% chance of being positive, and the best spinner
in the league about 0.44. If the last two seasons’ gap simply repeated,
the top-quartile spinner would be worth about 0.8 wins. These are modest
numbers and I would rather say so. Two things make them matter: the
margins (RCB missed the 2023 play-offs by one win and made 2024’s on net
run rate), and the price (the archetype is in the cheapest part of the
market).
6.2 Recommendation 1:
redeploy now, for free
Give the middle overs to the bowlers who are good at them, within the
four-over cap: more for Cameron Green, Mohammed Siraj and Glenn Maxwell,
fewer for the spinners at the bottom of the league list in this phase.
It is limited because those bowlers have other jobs, so it cannot be the
whole answer.
6.3 Recommendation 2: buy
one Indian middle-overs spinner, on several seasons of evidence
[TABLE]
Indian rather than overseas, because overseas players carry a large
price premium and each overseas slot spent on a spinner is a slot not
available for the batting that drives this team’s WAR. This is a list of
the archetype, not a shopping list. The right use of it is to set a
ceiling price per player from projected wins at about ₹6.0 crore per
WAR, and stop bidding there. If the auction pool is thin again, as it
was in December 2023, the trade window is the route.

to build around

The core that carried 2024 (Yash Dayal, Mohammed Siraj, Dinesh

6.5 What not to do
Don’t buy “bowling” in general. Powerplay and death
bowling were above average once the ground was accounted for, and the
2024 pace signings made the new ball even stronger. Another expensive
overseas fast bowler addresses a problem that is mostly
Chinnaswamy.
Don’t pay for clutch. It doesn’t repeat (r = -0.28
for batters, -0.24 for bowlers).
Don’t recruit spinners on one season. A single
season of middle-overs bowling is a weak guide to the next.
Don’t judge bowlers on raw economy at home. Use
venue-adjusted numbers in contract talks.
7 The decision
Decision. At the next auction, Royal Challengers
Bengaluru should spend on one Indian specialist spinner whose
shrunk middle-overs rate is in the league’s top quarter over more than
one season, with a bid ceiling set from projected wins at the
league’s going rate, and should move middle overs to its
existing positive options from the next match. It should not
spend its overseas slots or its largest bids on more pace bowling or on
players sold as finishers.
What it is worth. About 0.3 wins a season on
conservative, shrunk projections, and up to about 0.8 if the past two
seasons’ gap persisted. In 2023 one more win was the difference between
missing the play-offs and making them.
What would change my mind. If a third season showed
middle-overs spin back near average with the same bowlers, the
two-season pattern was noise and the money should go elsewhere. If
ball-tracking showed the spin problem was specific to Chinnaswamy’s
short square boundaries in a way the venue factor misses, the answer
would shift towards a different type of spinner rather than a
better one.
How to monitor it. Three numbers, every five
matches: middle-overs spin runs above average per 100 balls (target: at
least the league median); the share of middle-overs balls going to
bowlers with positive shrunk rates (target: more than half); and cost
per WAR against the league rate at season end.

8 Epilogue: what actually
happened next
The data stop in May 2024, and nothing below was used in the
analysis. At the November 2024 mega auction Royal Challengers Bengaluru
bought two spinners: Krunal Pandya for ₹5.75 crore and
Suyash Sharma for ₹2.6 crore (Sportskeeda;
Onmanorama).
Krunal Pandya is 3rd on the shortlist above, and Suyash Sharma is on the
2023 list of regular middle-overs spinners. RCB won the 2025 title,
beating Punjab by six runs in the final, and Krunal Pandya was player of
the match with 2 for 17 from his four overs (cricket.com).
One outcome proves nothing on its own, and many things changed in a
mega-auction year. But it is reassuring that the archetype this analysis
points to, from two seasons of ball-by-ball data, is the one a real
recruitment team went looking for a season later.
9 Limitations
Two seasons is not much. Player estimates are
heavily shrunk, and the gate-1 result for every team is uncertain under
resampling.
Auction prices are incomplete. Some replacement
signings are not on the auction sheets and are left unpriced; trades are
priced at the sheet value.
The audit is not causal. “Released player X did
well elsewhere” mixes his skill with a new team, role and ground.
The foresight market is thin. Only a handful of
auction-pool spinners had 2023 middle-overs evidence, so foresight
comparisons rest on small samples.
WAR is a description, at a stated replacement level
and runs-per-win rate. Other defensible choices would move the levels,
not much the rankings.
